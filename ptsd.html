THINGS TO KNOW

PTSD is a reaction to something overwhelming that happened to you. Your brain and body learned to stay on alert, and sometimes they stay that way longer than you want. Flashbacks, nightmares, anger, numbness, or avoiding certain places are common responses. Reacting this way does not mean you are weak.

The tips below are based on guidance from the PTSD service at Region Midtjylland. They can help you feel more in control, but they work best alongside professional help, not instead of it.

Content note: this page talks about trauma, anxiety and mental health. Take a break whenever you need one.

EVERYDAY TIPS

Stay close to people who make you feel safe

Pick one or two people you trust. You don't have to explain everything. Saying "I'm not doing well today" is enough to start.

Tell people what you need

Some people want to help but don't know how. Ask for practical help, or simply for someone to listen. Asking is not being a burden.

Talk about it when you are ready

Telling your story can make it feel less like a loop in your head. Share only as much as you want, and only when you choose to.

Learn your early warning signs

Notice what comes before a hard period, such as panic attacks, trouble remembering things, or changes in how you see yourself. If you still feel unwell a month after something difficult, that is a good time to ask for help.

Lower the bar for a while

You may have less energy than before, and that is okay. Plan one small thing a day. Use notes and calendar alerts so you don't have to remember everything.

Look after your body

Regular movement, regular meals, and a steady sleep routine help your body recover. A short walk counts.

Be careful with alcohol and drugs

They may feel like relief for a moment, but they can make symptoms worse and get in the way of treatment.

Ground yourself when it gets too much

Name 5 things you can see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, and 1 you can taste. It brings your attention back to the present moment.

Accept that this is hard

Strong feelings, tension, or restlessness are part of how the body reacts to trauma. They are not a personal failure, and you are allowed to make mistakes.

Getting professional help

Professional help is one of the most effective tools for PTSD. A talk with a psychologist or doctor can bring order to thoughts that feel scattered, and many people feel better afterwards.

1. Book a time with your doctor

Describe what you are experiencing, for example: "I keep having flashbacks and I can't sleep."

2. Ask about a referral

Ask about a psychologist, or a specialist clinic for trauma and PTSD.

3. Follow your medicine plan

If you are prescribed medicine, follow your doctor's advice. Don't stop taking it on your own. Talk to your doctor first about side effects or when it is safe to stop.

4. Ask again if needed

If the first person you speak to does not help, you can ask for a different doctor or clinic.

If someone close to you has PTSD

Listening often helps more than fixing. Ask what they need instead of guessing. Keep showing up, even if they pull back for a while. Family and friends can also learn how to respond at different stages, and they can get guidance from the health service too.

Where this comes from

The tips are based on "Livet med PTSD" from Region Midtjylland's health portal, sundhed.rm.dk. The text was updated in December 2025 and reviewed by a senior psychologist at the PTSD and Anxiety Clinic, Aarhus University Hospital.

Take it one step at a time

You don't have to do everything today. Choose one small thing, and let the rest wait.
